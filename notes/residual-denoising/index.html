Residual Denoising vs. Action-Space Residuals in Diffusion Policies

What changes when we move the correction inside the sampler?

Intro

A robot can know how to move a tray and still put it back before its teammate finishes wiping the table. This is the kind of problem I’ve been thinking about at ICON. The individual skills are there, but getting them to work together takes something more.

ALTER adds a learned correction to a frozen diffusion policy [5]. What I find interesting is where that correction goes: inside each denoising step. Adding it to the final action seems simpler. I wanted to work through what we actually gain by moving it inside.

Action-space residuals

Let \(o\) be one robot’s local observation and \(x\in\mathbb R^{Hd}\) an \(H\)-step chunk of \(d\)-dimensional actions [1]. An output residual modifies a completed sample:

\[ x_B\sim p_B(\cdot\mid o),\qquad x_A=x_B+r_\phi(o,x_B). \]

This follows the same idea as residual RL: keep a useful controller and learn what to add to it [2]. We could train this correction with RL or from demonstrations.

Suppose the correction only depends on \(o\). At a fixed observation, it’s a constant translation:

\[ p_A(x\mid o)=p_B(x-r_\phi(o)\mid o),\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(x_A\mid o)=\operatorname{Cov}(x_B\mid o). \]

We’ve moved the whole distribution over. Its shape stays the same. If the base chooses left or right equally often, adding the same number to both choices cannot turn that into “always right.”

But let the residual see the sampled action, and things change. For a toy base that outputs \(x_B\in\{-1,+1\}\), choosing \(r(x_B)=1-x_B\) sends both outcomes to \(+1\). An output residual can change the distribution’s shape too. It depends on what we let it use.

From a denoiser to a sampling direction

Start with a clean action chunk and add Gaussian noise: \(y=x+\sigma\epsilon\), where \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\). Here \(\sigma>0\) measures noise. A denoiser \(D(y,\sigma,o)\) tries to recover \(x\). Under squared-error training, its best possible answer is the mean of the clean chunks that could have produced \(y\) [3].

To see the connection to the score, differentiate the Gaussian convolution defining the noisy density \(p_\sigma\):

\[ \begin{aligned} s_\sigma(y\mid o) &=\nabla_y\log p_\sigma(y\mid o)\\ &=\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{x-y}{\sigma^2}\,\middle|\,y,o\right] =\frac{D^*(y,\sigma,o)-y}{\sigma^2}. \end{aligned} \]

This gradient is called the score. It points toward more likely noisy chunks. Using a learned \(D\) in place of \(D^*\) gives an estimate of it. We can then sample using the probability-flow ODE [3], [4]:

\[ \frac{dy}{d\sigma}=-\sigma s_\sigma(y\mid o) =\frac{y-D(y,\sigma,o)}{\sigma}. \]

We follow this equation from high noise toward zero, keeping \(o\) fixed. Different starting noise gives different action chunks. These are sampling steps; the robot hasn’t executed the chunk yet.

What residual denoising changes

Freeze \(D_B\) and learn \(D_R=D_B+\Delta_\phi\). ALTER’s adapter also receives the base prediction; I’ll leave that input implicit [5]. Substitution gives

\[ s_R=s_B+\frac{\Delta_\phi}{\sigma^2},\qquad \frac{dy_R}{d\sigma} =\frac{y_R-D_B(y_R,\sigma,o)}{\sigma} -\frac{\Delta_\phi(y_R,\sigma,o)}{\sigma}. \]

The residual now changes the direction the sampler follows at each noise level. These equalities hold for the score estimates even when the networks aren’t perfect.

The sign is easier to read after discretizing. For decreasing levels \(\sigma_k\), define \(c_k=(\sigma_k-\sigma_{k+1})/\sigma_k\). One Euler step becomes

\[ y_{k+1}=(1-c_k)y_k+c_kD_B(y_k,\sigma_k,o) +c_k\Delta_\phi(y_k,\sigma_k,o). \]

The actual step correction is scaled by \(c_k\). The minus sign above makes sense because we’re moving toward lower noise. I’m using Euler to keep the math short; ALTER uses EDM’s higher-order solver [3], [5]. Under our corruption rule, a noise predictor instead gives \(D=y-\sigma\hat\epsilon\), so its residual converts as \(\Delta_D=-\sigma\Delta_\epsilon\).

Why the final correction isn’t a simple sum

A correction also changes the input to the next denoising step. To track this, scale the residual by \(\eta\) and start both samplers from the same noise. Define \(v_k=\left.\partial y_k^\eta/\partial\eta\right|_{\eta=0}\), the sensitivity to turning on the residual. Differentiating the Euler update gives

\[ \begin{aligned} v_{k+1}&=J_kv_k+c_k\Delta_k,\\ J_k&=(1-c_k)I+c_k\nabla_yD_B(y_k^B,\sigma_k,o), \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\Delta_k\) is evaluated on the base path, and \(J_k\) measures how the base step responds to an input change. With \(v_0=0\), two steps give \(v_2=J_1c_0\Delta_0+c_1\Delta_1\). For small \(\eta\), the final change is approximately \(\eta v_K\), where \(K\) is the number of steps. Later steps can amplify, shrink, or rotate an earlier correction.

This matters when we say a residual is “small.” Bounding \(\|r\|\leq\varepsilon\) directly bounds the output change before clipping. Bounding \(\Delta_\phi\) gives no such bound by itself; we also need to control how the sampler responds. Neither gives a physical safety guarantee.

What is the residual learning?

Take collaborative demonstrations \((x,o)\sim\mathcal D_C\), add noise to their action chunks, and train only the adapter:

\[ \mathcal L_C=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D_C,\sigma,\epsilon} \left[w(\sigma)\left\|D_B(y,\sigma,o)+\Delta_\phi(y,\sigma,o)-x\right\|^2\right], \quad y=x+\sigma\epsilon. \]

Assume \(w(\sigma)>0\) and enough model capacity. The best residual is then \(\Delta^*=\mathbb E_C[x\mid y,\sigma,o]-D_B\). If the base is also an ideal denoiser, subtracting the two score identities gives

\[ \Delta^*(y,\sigma,o)=\sigma^2\nabla_y \log\frac{p_{C,\sigma}(y\mid o)}{p_{B,\sigma}(y\mid o)}. \]

I think this is the nicest way to read the residual: it learns how the collaborative score differs from the base score. That can change which action modes the sampler favors. And although training predicts a mean, that mean depends on the noisy example. Sampling can still produce several different behaviors.

One catch: freezing the base doesn’t automatically preserve its behavior. We’re still adding something to it! ALTER penalizes \(\|\Delta_\phi\|^2\) on replay inputs from the original tasks. Zero residual along every source sampling path recovers the base exactly; a small penalty on training examples doesn’t guarantee that [5].

Which would I use?

For small execution fixes, I’d start with an action-space residual. It adds one correction pass and can use a fresh observation after sampling. For learning changes to multimodal action chunks from demonstrations, I’d try residual denoising. We can train directly on noisy expert chunks and change how sampling unfolds, though we pay for the adapter at each denoiser evaluation.

That’s a choice about what’s easier to learn, not a proof that one can represent more than the other. A powerful sample-dependent output residual can also change the distribution. ALTER’s baselines don’t directly compare these two residual locations, so its results don’t settle which is better.

And for decentralized robots, both still work with what each robot can see. Moving the correction inside the sampler doesn’t tell it what its teammate is doing behind a box.
