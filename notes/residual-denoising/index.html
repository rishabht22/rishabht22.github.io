Residual Denoising vs. Action-Space Residuals in Diffusion Policies

Intro

Suppose two robots are cleaning a table. One moves a tray out of the way while the other wipes underneath it. Both know their part. But if the first puts the tray back too soon, the second can’t finish.

We could keep the pretrained policies frozen and learn a correction [5]. Adding it to the final action seems straightforward enough. Residual denoising puts it inside the sampling process instead, at every step. I wanted to see how moving the correction changes the actions we get.

Action-space residuals

Let \(o\) be the robot’s local observation and \(x\in\mathbb R^{Hd}\) a chunk of \(H\) actions, each with \(d\) coordinates [1]. Sample a chunk from the base policy \(p_B\), then add a correction.

\[ x_B\sim p_B(\cdot\mid o),\qquad x_A=x_B+r_\phi(o,x_B). \]

This is the idea behind residual RL. The controller already does something useful, and we learn what to add to it [2]. The correction could also be learned from demonstrations.

First, give the residual only \(o\). Once we fix the observation, every sampled chunk gets the same correction.

\[ p_A(x\mid o)=p_B(x-r_\phi(o)\mid o),\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(x_A\mid o)=\operatorname{Cov}(x_B\mid o). \]

The distribution shifts over, but keeps its shape. Say the base samples \(-1\) or \(+1\) equally often. Adding a constant can’t send both to \(+1\); the two outputs stay two units apart.

Now let the residual see the sample. Choosing \(r(x_B)=1-x_B\) sends both outputs to \(+1\). So an action-space residual can change the distribution’s shape. We have to be careful about which version we’re comparing.

From a denoiser to a sampling direction

Add Gaussian noise to a clean chunk to get \(y=x+\sigma\epsilon\), where \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\). The noise level \(\sigma>0\) controls how much we add. A denoiser \(D(y,\sigma,o)\) tries to recover \(x\). With squared-error training, its best answer is the posterior mean, the probability-weighted average of clean chunks that could have produced \(y\) [3].

Call the noisy density \(p_\sigma\). Differentiating its Gaussian convolution gives

\[ \begin{aligned} s_\sigma(y\mid o) &=\nabla_y\log p_\sigma(y\mid o)\\ &=\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{x-y}{\sigma^2}\,\middle|\,y,o\right] =\frac{D^*(y,\sigma,o)-y}{\sigma^2}. \end{aligned} \]

The gradient on the left is the score. It points toward more likely noisy chunks. A learned \(D\) gives us an estimate of it, which we can plug into the probability-flow ODE [3], [4].

\[ \frac{dy}{d\sigma}=-\sigma s_\sigma(y\mid o) =\frac{y-D(y,\sigma,o)}{\sigma}. \]

We follow this equation from high noise down toward zero. The observation stays fixed throughout sampling, and different starting noise gives different chunks. All of this happens before the robot executes the actions.

Putting the residual inside the sampler

Keep the base denoiser \(D_B\) frozen and learn \(D_R=D_B+\Delta_\phi\). The residual uses the noisy chunk, noise level, and local observation [5]. Put this into the score and ODE equations.

\[ s_R=s_B+\frac{\Delta_\phi}{\sigma^2},\qquad \frac{dy_R}{d\sigma} =\frac{y_R-D_B(y_R,\sigma,o)}{\sigma} -\frac{\Delta_\phi(y_R,\sigma,o)}{\sigma}. \]

We’re changing the direction the sampler follows as it builds the chunk. This algebra holds for the score estimates even if the networks aren’t perfect.

The minus sign is easier to follow in a discrete step. For decreasing noise levels \(\sigma_k\), set \(c_k=(\sigma_k-\sigma_{k+1})/\sigma_k\). An Euler step gives

\[ y_{k+1}=(1-c_k)y_k+c_kD_B(y_k,\sigma_k,o) +c_k\Delta_\phi(y_k,\sigma_k,o). \]

The correction enters as \(c_k\Delta_\phi\). It has a plus sign here because the step goes toward lower noise. Euler keeps the math short; we could use a higher-order solver too [3]. These equations use clean-action predictions. Under the same corruption rule, noise prediction gives \(D=y-\sigma\hat\epsilon\), so \(\Delta_D=-\sigma\Delta_\epsilon\).

Why the final correction isn’t a simple sum

After one correction, the next denoising step receives a different input. To track how that builds up, multiply the residual by \(\eta\) and start both samplers from the same noise. At \(\eta=0\), we have the base sampler. Define \(v_k=\left.\partial y_k^\eta/\partial\eta\right|_{\eta=0}\), which measures how the chunk starts changing as we turn the residual on. Differentiating gives

\[ \begin{aligned} v_{k+1}&=J_kv_k+c_k\Delta_k,\\ J_k&=(1-c_k)I+c_k\nabla_yD_B(y_k^B,\sigma_k,o), \end{aligned} \]

Both \(\Delta_k\) and \(J_k\) are evaluated on the base path. The matrix \(J_k\) tells us how an input change passes through the next base step. Since \(v_0=0\), two steps give \(v_2=J_1c_0\Delta_0+c_1\Delta_1\). The first correction has already passed through another step before reaching the output. That step can amplify, shrink, or rotate it. For small \(\eta\), the change after \(K\) steps is approximately \(\eta v_K\).

So “small residual” needs some care. Bounding \(\|r\|\leq\varepsilon\) directly bounds the output change before clipping. For a denoiser residual, we also need to know how the later steps respond. And a small action change by itself doesn’t guarantee safe robot motion.

What is the residual learning?

For training, take collaborative demonstrations \((x,o)\sim\mathcal D_C\), add noise to the chunks, and update only the adapter.

\[ \mathcal L_C=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D_C,\sigma,\epsilon} \left[w(\sigma)\left\|D_B(y,\sigma,o)+\Delta_\phi(y,\sigma,o)-x\right\|^2\right], \quad y=x+\sigma\epsilon. \]

With \(w(\sigma)>0\) and enough model capacity, the best residual is \(\Delta^*=\mathbb E_C[x\mid y,\sigma,o]-D_B\). If the base is also an ideal denoiser, we can subtract their score identities.

\[ \Delta^*(y,\sigma,o)=\sigma^2\nabla_y \log\frac{p_{C,\sigma}(y\mid o)}{p_{B,\sigma}(y\mid o)}. \]

I like this equation because it shows the ideal residual correcting the difference between the collaborative and base scores. That difference can change which action modes the sampler favors. Predicting a mean during training doesn’t force every final sample to be the same, either. The mean depends on the noisy input, which varies between samples.

The adapter can still ruin an old skill even though the base weights never moved. One way to discourage that is to penalize \(\|\Delta_\phi\|^2\) on replay inputs from the original tasks [5]. Zero residual along every source sampling path would recover the base exactly. A small penalty on training examples doesn’t guarantee zero residual on every path.

Which would I use?

If the plan is already good and the gripper needs a small position fix, I’d start with an action-space residual. It adds one correction pass and could use a fresh observation after sampling. If coordination changes the whole action chunk, I’d try residual denoising. We can train on noisy expert chunks with the denoising loss, though we pay for the adapter at every denoiser evaluation.

I wouldn’t call denoising better from this math alone. A powerful output residual that sees the sample can also change the distribution. I’d want to compare them using the same base policy, data, and compute budget before making that call.

Both still depend on the robot’s observations. Moving the correction inside the sampler won’t tell it what its teammate is doing behind a box.

Summary

Action-space residuals edit a finished chunk. Residual denoising changes the score by \(\Delta_\phi/\sigma^2\) while the chunk is being sampled, so later steps affect how the correction reaches the output. Both can change the action distribution; the better choice depends on the task and compute budget.
